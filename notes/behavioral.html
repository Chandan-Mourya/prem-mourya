Tell me about yourself must

I'm Chandan Mourya, a Senior Software Engineer with around 5 years of experience building scalable backend systems in Java, Spring Boot, and microservices. I'm currently at Indus Net Technologies, where I work on the Punctuations.ai platform — an AI-driven HR & recruitment product. There I built features like resume parsing, AI-powered candidate matching using embeddings, and a search layer on top of MongoDB and Elasticsearch handling thousands of profiles.

Before that, I was at OneXtel Media, working on telecom & messaging products — services for SMS, voice, and bulk campaigns at scale, integrating with operator APIs and Kafka. Across both roles I've also handled production debugging, AWS deployments, Docker/Kubernetes, and CI/CD. Outside work I've solved 450+ DSA problems and write engineering articles on Medium.

Walk me through your most recent project hot

The most recent one is Punctuations.ai at Indus Net — an AI hiring platform. Recruiters upload thousands of resumes, and we extract structured data, match candidates to job descriptions using embeddings, and let recruiters search via natural language. I designed the resume-parsing microservice using Spring Boot, with async processing through Kafka so heavy LLM calls don't block the API.

For storage I used MongoDB for the raw documents and Elasticsearch for searchable fields plus dense vectors for semantic match. The biggest engineering win was reducing match latency from 4 seconds to under 600 ms by caching JD embeddings in Redis and using kNN on Elasticsearch. We also added circuit breakers around the LLM provider so a vendor outage didn't take the platform down.

What's the most challenging problem you've solved?

At OneXtel we had an SMS-gateway service that started timing out during peak hours — about 5 p.m. when campaigns ran. From outside it looked like a database issue, but DB metrics were fine. I took thread dumps from the JVM and saw most threads stuck on a synchronized block around a connection pool we had wrapped for logging — a classic case of accidental lock contention.

I replaced the synchronized wrapper with HikariCP's native instrumentation and moved logging to an async appender. Throughput went from 200 to 1,200 messages per second on the same hardware, and p99 latency dropped from 4 s to 80 ms. That experience taught me to always reach for thread dumps and async-profiler before blaming the database.

Tell me about a conflict with a teammate

At Indus Net, a frontend teammate and I disagreed on whether the resume-upload API should return parsed fields synchronously or push to a queue. He wanted a synchronous response so the UI could show extracted data immediately; I argued an async pipeline was safer because parsing involved a 3-second LLM call and we couldn't hold an HTTP thread that long under load.

Instead of escalating, we built a quick prototype. I added a polling endpoint plus a WebSocket fallback, and he agreed once he saw the UX. We shipped the async approach and added a "parsing..." state. The lesson for me was that "disagree and prototype" beats "disagree and argue" — within a day we had data instead of opinions.

Describe a time you failed

Early in my OneXtel days I pushed a Kafka consumer change without enough load testing. I assumed the new consumer group would catch up quickly after deployment, but it processed messages 4× slower because I'd serialized JSON inefficiently. The lag piled up to 2 million messages overnight and woke up the on-call engineer.

I owned the rollback the same hour and stayed late to drain the backlog. After that I made load testing mandatory in our team's deployment checklist for any consumer change, and I added Grafana alerts on consumer lag. We never had that class of issue again. The takeaway: my speed of fixing it mattered, but preventing the next person from making the same mistake mattered more.

What's your biggest strength?

My biggest strength is going deep when something is broken. I don't stop at the first plausible explanation — I take thread dumps, read logs, write small reproducers, and validate the actual root cause. The SMS-gateway lock-contention bug at OneXtel is one example; another was a JVM memory leak at Indus Net that I traced to a ThreadLocal we never cleared inside a filter.

That habit pays off in two ways. First, we fix issues correctly the first time. Second, when I share the writeup, the team learns — I've written 4 internal post-mortems and a few Medium articles based on these debugging sessions. It's also why I enjoy mentoring juniors on debugging skills.

What's your biggest weakness?

Earlier in my career I struggled with over-engineering. I'd add abstractions and "future-proofing" before the product needed them — extra interfaces, generic frameworks, configuration files for things that never changed. It slowed down both me and reviewers.

I've been working on it by asking three questions before adding abstraction: is there a second caller today, is the change concrete or speculative, and can I refactor later in under an hour? Most of the time the simple version wins. I now ship smaller PRs, get faster reviews, and refactor only when the actual second use case appears. I still catch myself sometimes, but feedback in code reviews has improved noticeably.

How do you handle tight deadlines?

My approach has three steps. First, I cut scope with the product owner — I list what's strictly needed for the deadline versus what's nice-to-have, and we agree on a minimum cut. Second, I parallelize work — I unblock teammates by sharing API contracts and mock data first, so frontend and QA can start while I finish the backend.

Third, I'm honest about risk. If I sense we're slipping, I raise it early — not on the day before launch. At Punctuations.ai we had a 2-week deadline for the candidate-match feature; by cutting fancy filters from V1 and shipping a working core, we hit the date, and added the filters in V2 the next sprint. Cutting scope cleanly is usually cheaper than rushing low-quality code.

Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager

At Indus Net my manager wanted to add a new microservice for every feature we shipped. I felt we were creating distributed monoliths — too many tiny services tightly coupled through synchronous calls, all sharing the same database under the hood. Each one added deployment overhead without real isolation.

I prepared a one-pager comparing the existing approach with a modular monolith plus carve-outs only for the LLM-calling parts. I walked him through deployment time, on-call burden, and observability overhead with real numbers. He pushed back, we discussed for a week, and we settled on a hybrid: consolidate the CRUD-heavy services and keep async/LLM workloads as separate services. Result was faster deploys and clearer ownership.

Describe a time you took ownership beyond your role

At Indus Net we had recurring deploy failures — every Friday someone's PR would break the build because we had no consistent CI. It wasn't strictly my role, but I volunteered to fix it. I spent a week setting up GitHub Actions with a proper pipeline: lint, unit tests, integration tests against a Mongo + ES test container, then a staging deploy with smoke tests.

I wrote a short doc on how to use it and ran a 30-minute team session. After rollout, broken-build incidents dropped to nearly zero, deploys became boring (in a good way), and juniors felt safer pushing code. The work was outside my JD but it made the whole team faster — and it's now a standard pattern across our other repos.

How do you handle stress and burnout?

I treat stress as a signal, not a badge. When I notice it building up — usually as poor sleep or short patience — I take three actions. One: shrink the scope of what I'm trying to do in a day; I pick two or three real wins instead of ten. Two: I ask for help or escalate early — I've learned that silently struggling is worse for everyone than a 5-minute conversation with my manager.

Three: I protect non-work time. I do DSA practice and reading on weekends because they recharge me, not because I have to. After tough on-call weeks I take a real break. I've seen burnout slow people down for months — preventing it is part of being a senior engineer, not a luxury.
